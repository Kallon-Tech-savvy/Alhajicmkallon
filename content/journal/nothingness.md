Vividly, I can remember that in the last five years, I have repeatedly experienced feeling empty just as I am close to reaching a milestone. This made me ask some common critical questions about myself. Who am I? Who do I need to be? Why am I not who I need to be? How do my surroundings affect this? With these simple questions, I started enquiring into myself.

In this enquiry, I noticed that my own nature may not be so different from that of others. In our search for meaning, for something to signify, when we cannot find one, we sometimes feel empty. By empty, I mean unfulfilled, unhappy, or feeling unprogressive. If you think you do not feel emptiness, look again. Perhaps the accumulation of wealth, plentiful likes and comments on social platforms, influence and affluence can sometimes distract us from examining ourselves. But I challenge you to look again at yourself. How happy are you if you have lost your soul? How fulfilled are you without real peace? How progressive are you when you are mentally drained or losing yourself? Please look at yourself again and try to answer these questions and more.

In looking into myself and observing the world around me, I began to notice something else — man is erroneous. We sometimes mistake ourselves for being wise when we are not. We sometimes mistake ourselves for being something because we have status, wealth or power, when in reality, perhaps we are nothing more than travellers, and our stay here is temporary. With all the wealth, power and more, we will leave everything here again, just as we met them here. Looking at history, we can see how man has often built partial solutions out of the want for wealth, the want for power, the need to escape misery, or simply because his understanding was limited. Yet, like those before us, we too will leave behind everything, including our partial solutions.

And perhaps this is part of our struggle. We build collaborations, yet within ourselves, we sometimes feel threatened and want to pull out. We want to prove progress, we want to prove to everyone how happy we are, how fulfilled we are and how powerful we are. When we are unable to assert these, we may feel empty. By empty, I also mean not enough. Not enough to assert our power, not enough to show our influence. So we become more ambitious, pursuing that which we think is what we signify. And sometimes, in doing so, we lose ourselves, experience mental distress and other difficulties. For some of us, the fear of suffering may even push us towards doing evil rather than enduring hardship. Thus, we may end up selling our virtues for our ambitions.

But I have also been thinking about something. Is ambition itself the problem? I don't think so. We need ambition to build, to learn, to improve our conditions and sometimes to help others. There is nothing necessarily wrong with wanting a better life, acquiring wealth, or becoming influential. In fact, some of these things can help us serve others and solve problems that would otherwise remain unsolved. But what happens when our ambitions begin to control us? What happens when the desire to become somebody makes us forget who we already are?

Look at how easily we measure our lives against those of others. Someone gets a better job, another starts a business, another graduates, gets married, buys a car or builds a house. And before we even understand the circumstances surrounding their achievements, we begin questioning our own progress. Am I behind? Why haven't I achieved this? What am I doing with my life?

These are not necessarily bad questions. Sometimes they challenge us to become better. But sometimes we forget that we are comparing different people, different opportunities, different responsibilities and different journeys. We begin measuring our worth by things that may not even reflect what we truly want.

What troubles me is how easily we can become strangers to ourselves while trying to be recognised by others. We learn what makes people applaud, and gradually we begin living for that applause. We may even convince ourselves that we are happy because everyone around us believes we should be. Yet, when the applause stops and we are left alone with ourselves, some of those questions return.

Who am I without these things? What remains of me when I am no longer praised, recognised or needed for what I possess?

I am not suggesting that everyone who has wealth or influence is unhappy, nor that everyone who is struggling has lost meaning. That would be another error in my own judgement. What I am questioning is the belief that acquiring these things automatically gives our lives meaning. They may bring comfort, opportunities and even fulfilment, but they cannot answer every question we have about ourselves.

And this brings me back to my own experience.

Why have I sometimes felt empty even when I was close to achieving something important to me? Perhaps because I had already imagined that reaching that milestone would make me feel complete. I had given the achievement a responsibility it might never have been able to fulfil. I expected progress to settle questions that were not entirely about progress.

I am still enquiring into this. I cannot confidently tell you that I have found a permanent answer, because I haven't. There are still moments when I question myself, my direction and whether what I am doing is enough. But I am beginning to understand that not every feeling of emptiness means I have failed. Sometimes it may be a reason to examine what I am pursuing, why I am pursuing it and what I am sacrificing along the way.

Perhaps we also need to question our understanding of progress. Must progress always be something others can see? What about becoming more patient, more understanding, more responsible and more honest with ourselves? What about learning to accept what we do not know? What about the courage to abandon an ambition that no longer serves the person we want to become?

I think these things deserve consideration too.

We often speak of building a better future, but rarely ask what kind of people we are becoming while building it. We want successful businesses, respected careers, powerful institutions and comfortable lives. These are reasonable things to pursue. But if we gain them at the expense of our peace, our relationships and the virtues we once believed in, then we should at least have the courage to question the price we paid.

And when I say we are nothing more than travellers, I do not mean that our lives have no value. I mean that our time here is limited. Our possessions are temporary, our positions can change, and even the things we build may one day be forgotten or replaced. But perhaps that is another reason to be conscious of how we live, rather than a reason to believe that nothing matters.

We may not control how long we stay here or how much of our work will remain after us. But we can pay attention to how we treat people, what we contribute, the problems we choose to solve and the principles we are willing to stand by.

So, if you are reading this and you sometimes feel empty, I am not going to tell you to stop being ambitious or pretend that everything will suddenly make sense. I would rather ask you to examine yourself.

What are you pursuing? Why does it matter to you? Is it something you genuinely want, or something you have come to believe you must possess to be respected? And if you finally obtain it, will you still recognise the person you have become?

You may not have answers to all these questions today. I certainly do not have answers to all of mine. But I believe there is value in asking them, especially when we become so occupied with life that we forget to examine how we are living it.

I am learning that being enough does not mean refusing to grow, and being ambitious does not mean I must always feel inadequate. We can acknowledge what we have not achieved without dismissing what we have become. We can desire more without allowing that desire to consume everything we already have.

Perhaps the lesson is not that we should abandon our search for meaning, but that we should be careful not to lose ourselves in the search.

Because one day, the milestones we worry about, the wealth we accumulate, the influence we struggle to maintain and the recognition we seek may no longer matter as much as they do today.

And when that day comes, what will remain of the person who spent so much time pursuing them?

I leave you with this thought, just as I leave it with myself:

**Perhaps nothingness is not always the absence of something in our lives. Sometimes, it is what we feel when we have spent so much time trying to become somebody that we have forgotten to be ourselves.**
